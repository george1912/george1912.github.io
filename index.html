01 / THE FIRST 30 MINUTES

First period of reactivity

The newborn is typically alert and active at first. Heart rate may briefly reach 160–180 bpm, then gradually settles as activity decreases.

0

BIRTH

Warm, dry, stimulate. Rapidly assess breathing, tone, and heart rate.

1

MINUTE

Record the first APGAR snapshot. Do not wait for it to begin needed care.

5

MINUTES

Score again. The change helps describe the response to transition or support.

30

MINUTES

Heart rate and motor activity generally settle toward baseline.

!
STUDY NOTE

Use 160–180 bpm for the early peak—not 280 bpm. A rate of 280 is not an expected newborn transition finding and needs urgent evaluation.

03 / READ THE TOTAL

What does the score mean?

These bands describe the 5-minute score in term and late-preterm newborns. The trend from 1 to 5 minutes matters.

7–10Reassuring
4–6Moderately abnormal
0–3Low
⚑CLINICAL ANCHOR

APGAR describes status and response. It does not decide whether to start resuscitation, and it does not diagnose asphyxia or predict an individual child’s neurologic outcome.

IF THE 5-MINUTE SCORE IS < 7 → REPEAT EVERY 5 MINUTES, UP TO 20 MINUTES.

MODULE 02 / PHYSIOLOGIC ADAPTATION

First breath.
New rules.

The placenta clocks out. The lungs, circulation, temperature control, blood, gut, kidneys, liver, and immune defenses all begin working in a new way.

01

RESPIRATORY + CIRCULATION

The first breath flips the circuit.

1LABOR + BIRTH

Catecholamines and pressure changes help lung fluid move into blood vessels and lymphatics.

2AIR ENTERS

Temperature, touch, rising CO₂, and falling O₂ stimulate the first breaths.

3ALVEOLI OPEN

Surfactant lowers surface tension so the air sacs stay open after exhalation.

4FLOW CHANGES

Pulmonary resistance falls. Blood now travels to the lungs for oxygen.

VAGINAL BIRTH

Labor helps clear fluid.

Chest compression contributes, but hormonal signals and reabsorption do most of the clearing. Think: labor + squeeze + reabsorb.

CESAREAN WITHOUT LABOR

Fluid may linger longer.

Less labor signaling can delay fluid clearance, increasing risk for transient tachypnea. Route affects transition—not the baby’s worth or outcome.

EXPECTED
30–60BREATHS / MIN · COUNT A FULL MINUTE AT REST
  • Rhythm may be “regularly irregular.”
  • Brief pauses of about 5–10 seconds can occur.
  • Symmetric chest movement; no persistent work of breathing.
  • A strong cry shows air is moving.
RESPIRATORY DISTRESS
GRUNTINGNASAL FLARINGRETRACTIONSCENTRAL CYANOSIS

APNEA = ≥20 seconds, or a shorter pause with bradycardia, cyanosis, pallor, or desaturation. This is not normal periodic breathing.

PLACENTA OFF → LUNGS ON

Three fetal shortcuts begin to close.

Clamping the cord raises systemic resistance. Lung expansion lowers pulmonary resistance. More blood reaches the lungs, and left atrial pressure rises above right atrial pressure.

FOForamen ovale

Functional flap closes as left atrial pressure becomes higher.

DADuctus arteriosus

Higher oxygen and lower prostaglandins trigger constriction.

DVDuctus venosus

Cord clamping ends umbilical flow; the liver now receives the blood.

02

THERMOREGULATION

Warmth is oxygen-saving care.

AXILLARY TARGET36.5–37.4°C97.7–99.3°F

Temperature is not part of APGAR. It is a separate vital sign and a priority during transition.

◆

No effective shivering.

The newborn makes heat mainly through brown-fat metabolism—nonshivering thermogenesis. This costs glucose and oxygen.

C

Convection

Baby → cooler air

Wrap + hat. Avoid drafts. Keep the room warm.

R

Radiation

Baby → nearby cold object

Move away from cold windows/walls; use a radiant warmer.

E

Evaporation

Wet skin → vapor

Dry immediately. Remove every wet blanket or linen.

C

Conduction

Baby → cold surface

Warm the scale, mattress, hands, and blankets first.

!COLD-STRESS CASCADE
COLD→O₂ + GLUCOSE USE ↑→VASOCONSTRICTION→HYPOXEMIA→METABOLIC ACIDOSIS

As oxygen drops and acid rises, pulmonary vessels may constrict—sending less blood to the lungs and worsening the cycle. Dry + warm + skin-to-skin breaks the chain early.

04

HEMOSTASIS

Vitamin K helps the bleeding stop.

WHY LOW?

Little crosses the placenta, body stores are small, breast milk contains little, and the gut microbiome is still developing.

WHAT’S AFFECTED?

Vitamin K–dependent clotting factors are not activated well, increasing bleeding risk.

THE FIX

Routine intramuscular vitamin K after birth prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding.

05

GASTROINTESTINAL

Feed small. Track the diaper.

GUTMICROBIOME STARTS NOW

The gut is rapidly colonized after birth. The newborn does not yet make enough vitamin K from gut bacteria.

STOMACHSMALL CAPACITY

Frequent, small feeds fit newborn physiology. The “30–90 mL” note describes feeding volume—not stool capacity—and varies with age.

0–2DAYS

Meconium

Black–green · thick · tarry

Amniotic fluid, bile pigments, and shed cells. Expect passage within 24–48 hours.

2–3DAYS

Transitional

Green–brown → yellow–brown

Thinner and less sticky as feeding moves milk through the gut.

4+DAYS

Milk stool

Feeding pattern appears

Breast milk: yellow, loose, seedy. Formula: tan/brown, firmer, stronger odor.

K

Kidneys

  • Low GFR and limited ability to concentrate urine
  • Limited bicarbonate handling
  • Extra extracellular fluid contributes to early weight loss
  • Expect first void within 24 hours
G

Glucose

  • Cord clamp ends the constant maternal glucose supply
  • Cold stress and poor feeding drain glycogen faster
  • Infant of a diabetic mother: high insulin → hypoglycemia
  • Monitor and feed per protocol
I

Immunity

  • Own immune response is immature—not absent
  • Maternal IgG crossed the placenta
  • Colostrum supplies secretory IgA
  • Skin and gut barriers are still adapting

MODULE 03 / BASELINE ASSESSMENT

Look. Measure. Listen.

Begin while the newborn is quiet. Observe first, then measure, then use your hands and stethoscope. The calm baby gives the clearest baseline.

1LOOKcolor · effort · tone
2MEASUREweight · length · temperature
3LISTENheart · respirations · one full minute

EXPECTED TRANSITION

Pink center; blue hands and feet may occur.

Acrocyanosis is peripheral color change and is common during the first 24–48 hours.

REPORT PROMPTLY

Blue lips, tongue, or trunk are different.

Central cyanosis, persistent pallor, or gray color—especially with breathing or feeding difficulty—needs evaluation.

MEMORY: Hands + feet may be blue. Lips + tongue should not.

MEASURE / THE OUTER NUMBERS

MODULE 04 / VISUAL + PHYSICAL ASSESSMENT

Notice the pattern—not one feature.

Move in the same order every time. Compare right with left, observe at rest and during crying, and interpret each finding with the rest of the exam.

FACE→HEAD→SKIN→TRUNK→LIMBS
01

FACE

Pair the structures. Watch the movement.

EYES + FACESymmetric at rest and while crying

Check eye size and position, mouth movement, and whether one side droops. Asymmetry can reflect positioning, swelling, birth injury, facial-nerve weakness, or a congenital difference—it is not a genetic diagnosis by itself.

EARSSame level, shape, and position

Note low-set or asymmetric ears, preauricular tags or pits, and cartilage recoil. Confirm the newborn hearing screen is completed.

NOSEMidline + two patent nares

Observe airflow through each nostril. Patency matters because newborns primarily breathe through the nose.

MOUTHPink, moist, mobile, intact

Inspect the lips for a cleft; use a gloved finger to palpate the hard and soft palate. Note tongue movement, frenulum, suck, and facial symmetry.

EP

Epstein pearls: tiny, firm white keratin cysts on the palate or gums. Benign and self-resolving.

NT

Natal teeth: uncommon but possible. Assess stability and feeding or aspiration risk—do not assume every newborn must be toothless.

MEMORY: Two ears · two nares · one intact palate.

02

HEAD + SCALP

Shape changes tell you where the fluid sits.

HEAD CIRCUMFERENCE32–37 cm

Measure above the eyebrows and ears around the largest occipital prominence.

HEAD VS CHESTHEAD ≈ 2 cm LARGER

Chest averages about 33 cm. Interpret size with gestational age, molding, growth pattern, and the full exam.

Anterior fontanel

Diamond-shaped; soft and flat when calm. Common closure range is roughly 13–24 months.

Posterior fontanel

Small and triangular; commonly closes around 6–8 weeks.

CONTEXT MATTERS

Crying can make a fontanel look briefly full. A persistent tense or bulging fontanel while calm needs evaluation; a sunken fontanel can suggest dehydration.

MOLDINGSkull bones overlap

Shape change from birth pressure—not peeling skin. Usually improves over the first 24–48 hours.

CAPUTEdema crosses sutures

Soft, boggy scalp swelling present at birth. Usually resolves within a few days.

CEPHALOBlood stays inside sutures

Subperiosteal blood collection; does not cross suture lines. May take 2–6 weeks and increases jaundice risk.

SUBGALEALDiffuse + expanding = emergency

Boggy swelling can cross sutures and spread widely. Pallor, tachycardia, or increasing head size demands immediate escalation.

MEMORY: Caput crosses. Cephalo is contained.

03

SKIN

Name it. Document it. Reassure appropriately.

DRYDesquamation

Dry peeling is common, especially in later-term newborns. Deep cracking supports greater maturity; do not pull loose skin.

BLUE-GRAYCongenital dermal melanocytosis

Often over the sacrum, buttocks, or back. Document size and location because it can resemble bruising; usually fades over years. This replaces the outdated term “Mongolian spots.”

WHITE DOTSMilia

Tiny facial keratin cysts, often on the nose or chin. Not acne; no squeezing or treatment.

RED + WHITEErythema toxicum

Benign blotchy newborn rash with small papules or pustules, usually sparing palms and soles. Resolves without treatment.

FINE HAIRLanugo

Soft fetal hair over the back, shoulders, face, or scalp. More noticeable with prematurity and fades after birth.

WHITE COATINGVernix caseosa

Protective, creamy coating from fetal life. Amount varies with gestational age; it is not an infection.

PINK PATCHNevus simplex

“Stork bite” or “salmon patch”: flat, blanching pink area on eyelids, glabella, or nape; more visible with crying and commonly fades.

HALF REDHarlequin color change

Dependent half becomes red while the upper half pales, divided at midline. A brief episode that resolves is usually benign autonomic immaturity.

LACYCutis marmorata

Mottled, marbled skin with cold exposure. It should improve with warming; persistence or illness signs require evaluation.

RAISED REDInfantile hemangioma

Vascular growth may enlarge before gradually involuting. Location, size, ulceration, or interference with vision/airway can require referral.

04

CHEST + ABDOMEN

Symmetric chest. Soft belly. Three-vessel cord.

CHESTCylindrical + symmetric

AP diameter is close to the transverse diameter. Measure at the nipple line; average circumference is about 33 cm, usually around 2 cm less than the head. Palpate clavicles for crepitus or tenderness.

ABDOMENRound, soft, and moving

The abdomen rises with breathing. Inspect for distention, visible vessels, masses, or hernia; listen for bowel sounds. Abdominal circumference is trended when clinically indicated.

CORDAVA

Two Arteries + one Vein. Confirm the clamp and vessel count. The stump should dry without spreading redness, purulent drainage, foul odor, or persistent bleeding.

MEMORY: AVA = Artery · Vein · Artery.

05

EXTREMITIES

MOVE: motion, one length, visible digits, equal folds.

MMotion

Expect spontaneous, symmetric movement and a flexed posture. Limited or one-sided movement needs follow-up.

OOne length

Compare limb length, position, range of motion, and whether a foot is flexible or fixed.

VVisible digits

Count fingers and toes; note polydactyly, syndactyly, nail development, and hand or foot alignment.

EEqual folds

Compare thigh and gluteal folds and leg movement. Hip assessment is performed with trained technique.

Single transverse palmar crease

Can occur normally. It is associated with several conditions, including trisomy 21, but it never makes the diagnosis alone. Interpret it with the full exam.

Plantar creases

Sole creases increase with gestational maturity and are one element of gestational-age assessment—not a stand-alone finding.
