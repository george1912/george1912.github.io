MODULE 02 / PHYSIOLOGIC ADAPTATION

First breath.
New rules.

The placenta clocks out. The lungs, circulation, temperature control, blood, gut, kidneys, liver, and immune defenses all begin working in a new way.

01

RESPIRATORY + CIRCULATION

The first breath flips the circuit.

1LABOR + BIRTH

Catecholamines and pressure changes help lung fluid move into blood vessels and lymphatics.

2AIR ENTERS

Temperature, touch, rising CO₂, and falling O₂ stimulate the first breaths.

3ALVEOLI OPEN

Surfactant lowers surface tension so the air sacs stay open after exhalation.

4FLOW CHANGES

Pulmonary resistance falls. Blood now travels to the lungs for oxygen.

VAGINAL BIRTH

Labor helps clear fluid.

Chest compression contributes, but hormonal signals and reabsorption do most of the clearing. Think: labor + squeeze + reabsorb.

CESAREAN WITHOUT LABOR

Fluid may linger longer.

Less labor signaling can delay fluid clearance, increasing risk for transient tachypnea. Route affects transition—not the baby’s worth or outcome.

EXPECTED
30–60BREATHS / MIN · COUNT A FULL MINUTE AT REST
  • Rhythm may be “regularly irregular.”
  • Brief pauses of about 5–10 seconds can occur.
  • Symmetric chest movement; no persistent work of breathing.
  • A strong cry shows air is moving.
RESPIRATORY DISTRESS
GRUNTINGNASAL FLARINGRETRACTIONSCENTRAL CYANOSIS

APNEA = ≥20 seconds, or a shorter pause with bradycardia, cyanosis, pallor, or desaturation. This is not normal periodic breathing.

TTNSUPPORT · WARM · WATCH · WAIT

Transient tachypnea = delayed fetal lung-fluid clearance. Maintain warmth, position and clear the airway only as needed, monitor work of breathing and pulse oximetry, and give oxygen or CPAP only as ordered for the infant’s need. Most cases improve within 2–3 days; worsening distress requires prompt evaluation for other causes.

PLACENTA OFF → LUNGS ON

Three fetal shortcuts begin to close.

Clamping the cord raises systemic resistance. Lung expansion lowers pulmonary resistance. More blood reaches the lungs, and left atrial pressure rises above right atrial pressure.

DADuctus arteriosus

DVDuctus venosus

Cord clamping ends umbilical flow; the liver now receives the blood.

02

THERMOREGULATION

Warmth is oxygen-saving care.

AXILLARY TARGET36.5–37.4°C97.7–99.3°F

Temperature is not part of APGAR. It is a separate vital sign and a priority during transition.

◆

No effective shivering.

The newborn makes heat mainly through brown-fat metabolism—nonshivering thermogenesis. This costs glucose and oxygen.

C

Convection

Baby → cooler air

Wrap + hat. Avoid drafts. Keep the room warm.

R

Radiation

Baby → nearby cold object

Move away from cold windows/walls; use a radiant warmer.

E

Evaporation

Wet skin → vapor

Dry immediately. Remove every wet blanket or linen.

C

Conduction

Baby → cold surface

Warm the scale, mattress, hands, and blankets first.

!COLD-STRESS CASCADE
COLD→O₂ + GLUCOSE USE ↑→VASOCONSTRICTION→HYPOXEMIA→METABOLIC ACIDOSIS

As oxygen drops and acid rises, pulmonary vessels may constrict—sending less blood to the lungs and worsening the cycle. Dry + warm + skin-to-skin breaks the chain early.

04

HEMOSTASIS

Vitamin K helps the bleeding stop.

WHY LOW?

Little crosses the placenta, body stores are small, breast milk contains little, and the gut microbiome is still developing.

WHAT’S AFFECTED?

Vitamin K–dependent clotting factors are not activated well, increasing bleeding risk.

THE FIX

Routine intramuscular vitamin K after birth prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding.

05

GASTROINTESTINAL

Feed small. Track the diaper.

GUTMICROBIOME STARTS NOW

The gut is rapidly colonized after birth. The newborn does not yet make enough vitamin K from gut bacteria.

STOMACHSMALL CAPACITY

Frequent, small feeds fit newborn physiology. The “30–90 mL” note describes feeding volume—not stool capacity—and varies with age.

MODULE 03 / BASELINE ASSESSMENT

Look. Measure. Listen.

Begin while the newborn is quiet. Observe first, then measure, then use your hands and stethoscope. The calm baby gives the clearest baseline.

1LOOKcolor · effort · tone
2MEASUREweight · length · temperature
3LISTENheart · respirations · one full minute

EXPECTED TRANSITION

Pink center; blue hands and feet may occur.

Acrocyanosis is peripheral color change and is common during the first 24–48 hours.

REPORT PROMPTLY

Blue lips, tongue, or trunk are different.

Central cyanosis, persistent pallor, or gray color—especially with breathing or feeding difficulty—needs evaluation.

MODULE 04 / VISUAL + PHYSICAL ASSESSMENT

Notice the pattern—not one feature.

Move in the same order every time. Compare right with left, observe at rest and during crying, and interpret each finding with the rest of the exam.

FACE→HEAD→SKIN→TRUNK→LIMBS
01

FACE

Pair the structures. Watch the movement.

EYES + FACESymmetric at rest and while crying

Check eye size and position, mouth movement, and whether one side droops. Asymmetry can reflect positioning, swelling, birth injury, facial-nerve weakness, or a congenital difference—it is not a genetic diagnosis by itself.

EARSSame level, shape, and position

Note low-set or asymmetric ears, preauricular tags or pits, and cartilage recoil. Confirm the newborn hearing screen is completed.

NOSEMidline + two patent nares

Observe airflow through each nostril. Patency matters because newborns primarily breathe through the nose.

MOUTHPink, moist, mobile, intact

Inspect the lips for a cleft; use a gloved finger to palpate the hard and soft palate. Note tongue movement, frenulum, suck, and facial symmetry.

EP

Epstein pearls: tiny, firm white keratin cysts on the palate or gums. Benign and self-resolving.

NT

Natal teeth: uncommon but possible. Assess stability and feeding or aspiration risk—do not assume every newborn must be toothless.

MEMORY: Two ears · two nares · one intact palate.

02

HEAD + SCALP

Shape changes tell you where the fluid sits.

HEAD CIRCUMFERENCE32–37 cm

Measure above the eyebrows and ears around the largest occipital prominence.

HEAD VS CHESTHEAD ≈ 2 cm LARGER

Chest averages about 33 cm. Interpret size with gestational age, molding, growth pattern, and the full exam.

Anterior fontanel

Diamond-shaped; soft and flat when calm. Common closure range is roughly 13–24 months.

Posterior fontanel

Small and triangular; commonly closes around 6–8 weeks.

CONTEXT MATTERS

Crying can make a fontanel look briefly full. A persistent tense or bulging fontanel while calm needs evaluation; a sunken fontanel can suggest dehydration.

MICROSmaller than expected

Plot head circumference for gestational age and trend it. Microcephaly describes a measurement pattern—not a cause.

MACROLarger than expected

Recheck technique, compare with family pattern, and assess growth trend, sutures, fontanels, and neurologic findings.

FONTANELLarge · small · closed early

Size varies. An unusually large, small, or apparently closed fontanel is a cue for a complete exam and follow-up—not a stand-alone diagnosis.

MOLDINGSkull bones overlap

Shape change from birth pressure—not peeling skin. Usually improves over the first 24–48 hours.

CAPUTEdema crosses sutures

Soft, boggy scalp swelling present at birth. Usually resolves within a few days.

CEPHALOBlood stays inside sutures

Subperiosteal blood collection; does not cross suture lines. May take 2–6 weeks and increases jaundice risk.

SUBGALEALDiffuse + expanding = emergency

Boggy swelling can cross sutures and spread widely. Pallor, tachycardia, or increasing head size demands immediate escalation.

MEMORY: Caput crosses. Cephalo is contained.

03

SKIN

Name it. Document it. Reassure appropriately.

DRYDesquamation

Dry peeling is common, especially in later-term newborns. Deep cracking supports greater maturity; do not pull loose skin.

BLUE-GRAYCongenital dermal melanocytosis

Often over the sacrum, buttocks, or back. Document size and location because it can resemble bruising; usually fades over years. This replaces the outdated term “Mongolian spots.”

WHITE DOTSMilia

Tiny facial keratin cysts, often on the nose or chin. Not acne; no squeezing or treatment.

RED + WHITEErythema toxicum

Benign blotchy newborn rash with small papules or pustules, usually sparing palms and soles. Resolves without treatment.

FINE HAIRLanugo

Soft fetal hair over the back, shoulders, face, or scalp. More noticeable with prematurity and fades after birth.

WHITE COATINGVernix caseosa

Protective, creamy coating from fetal life. Amount varies with gestational age; it is not an infection.

PINK PATCHNevus simplex

“Stork bite” or “salmon patch”: flat, blanching pink area on eyelids, glabella, or nape; more visible with crying and commonly fades.

HALF REDHarlequin color change

Dependent half becomes red while the upper half pales, divided at midline. A brief episode that resolves is usually benign autonomic immaturity.

LACYCutis marmorata

Mottled, marbled skin with cold exposure. It should improve with warming; persistence or illness signs require evaluation.

RAISED REDInfantile hemangioma

Vascular growth may enlarge before gradually involuting. Location, size, ulceration, or interference with vision/airway can require referral.

04

CHEST + ABDOMEN

Symmetric chest. Soft belly. Three-vessel cord.

CHESTCylindrical + symmetric

AP diameter is close to the transverse diameter. Measure at the nipple line; average circumference is about 33 cm, usually around 2 cm less than the head. Palpate clavicles for crepitus or tenderness.

ABDOMENRound, soft, and moving

The abdomen rises with breathing. Inspect for distention, visible vessels, masses, or hernia; listen for bowel sounds. Abdominal circumference is trended when clinically indicated.

CORDAVA

Two Arteries + one Vein. Confirm the clamp and vessel count. The stump should dry without spreading redness, purulent drainage, foul odor, or persistent bleeding.

MEMORY: AVA = Artery · Vein · Artery.

05

EXTREMITIES

MOVE: motion, one length, visible digits, equal folds.

MMotion

Expect spontaneous, symmetric movement and a flexed posture. Limited or one-sided movement needs follow-up.

OOne length

Compare limb length, position, range of motion, and whether a foot is flexible or fixed.

VVisible digits

Count fingers and toes; note polydactyly, syndactyly, nail development, and hand or foot alignment.

EEqual folds

Compare thigh and gluteal folds and leg movement. Hip assessment is performed with trained technique.

Single transverse palmar crease

Can occur normally. It is associated with several conditions, including trisomy 21, but it never makes the diagnosis alone. Interpret it with the full exam.

Plantar creases

Sole creases increase with gestational maturity and are one element of gestational-age assessment—not a stand-alone finding.

MODULE 05 / MOVEMENT + REFLEXES

Move. Reflex. Sense. Examine.

Newborn bones contain more cartilage and are more flexible than adult bones. Read the whole pattern: posture, symmetric movement, reflexes, state, senses, and anatomy.

MOVE→REFLEX→SENSE→EXAMINE
01

SKELETAL + HIPS

Compare the sides before naming the sign.

CARTILAGE FIRST

Expect a flexed posture and spontaneous, symmetric movement. Compare limb position, length, alignment, range of motion, hands, feet, and digits. Pain, fixed deformity, or unequal movement needs follow-up.

FFolds

Compare gluteal and thigh folds. Asymmetry is a clue, but folds alone do not diagnose DDH.

AAbduction

With hips flexed, compare how evenly and freely each hip opens. Limited or asymmetric abduction is concerning.

KKnees

Galeazzi (Allis) sign: with hips and knees flexed, unequal knee heights suggest apparent femoral shortening.

MManeuvers

Ortolani gently reduces a dislocated hip; Barlow assesses instability. These are trained-examiner maneuvers—never force them.

MEMORY: FAKM = Folds · Abduction · Knees · Maneuvers.

FOOTClubfoot = talipes equinovarus

The foot points down and inward. Note whether the position is flexible or fixed; a fixed deformity needs evaluation.

DIGITSPoly = extra · Syn = together

Polydactyly means extra fingers or toes. Syndactyly means webbed or fused digits. Document side and function.

TERM CLEANUP

“Gilles,” “Glasey,” and “alias” in spoken notes most likely refer to the Galeazzi sign, also called the Allis sign.

02

NEUROMUSCULAR

Stimulus in. Symmetric response out.

Primitive reflexes are automatic. Check the response on both sides and interpret it with tone, alertness, gestational age, and the full neurologic exam.

ROOTCheek → turn + open

Stroke the cheek or mouth corner; the newborn turns toward the stimulus and opens the mouth. Usually integrates around 3–4 months.

SUCKMouth → suck + swallow

Assess strength and coordination. A weak or absent response can reflect prematurity, illness, medication, or neurologic depression.

HOLDPalm/sole → flex

Palmar grasp closes the hand around a finger; plantar grasp flexes the toes. Compare both sides.

STARTLEOut → then in

Moro response: arms abduct and extend, fingers open, then arms return inward. It should be symmetric and usually integrates by 4–6 months.

FANSole → great toe up

Babinski response: stroke the lateral sole; the great toe extends and the others fan. Expected in infancy and may remain until about age 2.

STEPSurface → alternate

Support the newborn upright with soles touching a surface; alternating stepping movements appear. Usually fades around 2 months.

FENCEHead turn → pose

Tonic neck: face-side arm and leg extend while the opposite side flexes. Usually integrates by 4–6 months.

ROOT · SUCK · HOLD · STARTLE · FAN · STEP · FENCE

Absent, clearly asymmetric, unusually weak, or persistent reflexes are findings to interpret and report—not diagnoses by themselves.

04

REPRODUCTIVE + GENITOURINARY

Openings. Swelling. Symmetry.

Inspect gently near the end of the exam. Maternal hormones can create temporary findings that look surprising but are often expected.

FEMALEEdema, tag, discharge

Inspect labia, clitoris, hymenal opening, and anus. Mild swelling, a hymenal/vaginal tag, white mucus, or a small blood-tinged discharge called pseudomenstruation can occur from maternal hormone withdrawal.

BOTHBreast tissue may swell

Maternal hormones can cause temporary breast enlargement and a few drops of milky discharge in any newborn. Do not squeeze. Note redness, warmth, or tenderness.

MILK LINESupernumerary nipple

An extra nipple may appear anywhere along the embryologic milk line. This is the likely term behind “supernumerary lipoma.” Document the location.

MALEMeatus, foreskin, testes

Meatus should be at the tip: hypospadias = ventral; epispadias = dorsal. Defer circumcision until evaluated. Newborn foreskin is normally nonretractile—never force it. Palpate for both testes; undescended testes need follow-up.

MEMORY: Openings · swelling · symmetry—and never force the foreskin.

CORD + PROCEDURE SITES

Clean. Dry. Observe.

02

PREVENT + SCREEN

Right baby. Right intervention. Right follow-up.

VITAMIN KOne IM dose after birth

Prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding. Current AAP guidance supports administration within 6 hours, after initial bonding/feeding when appropriate. Follow the ordered weight-based product dose and facility policy.

EYE OINTMENTErythromycin to both eyes

Gonococcal ophthalmia prophylaxis is given as soon as possible after birth. Tetracycline ointment is no longer manufactured in the United States.

HEPATITIS BMaternal status drives urgency

HBsAg positive or unknown: vaccine within 12 hours; add HBIG when indicated. HBsAg negative: current U.S. guidance uses parent–clinician shared decision-making for the birth dose. Follow the current schedule and facility policy.

3 UNIVERSAL SCREENSBlood spot · hearing · CCHD pulse ox

State panels vary. Blood-spot screening includes disorders such as PKU; it is not a PKU-only test.

WHEN INDICATEDGlucose · blood type/DAT · bilirubin

Glucose heel sticks are for risk factors or symptoms—not every newborn every hour. Blood type and direct antiglobulin testing depend on maternal/newborn risk. TcB screens bilirubin; TSB guides treatment.

DOCUMENTTime · result · response

Record consent or decline, medication/vaccine details, specimen time, screening result, repeat plan, teaching, and required referral.

03

Use the curve—not a memorized table.

Fixed “normal bilirubin by day” tables do not decide phototherapy. Plot the value with the current guideline or facility tool; use serum bilirubin for treatment decisions.

Use ordered overhead lights or a fiber-optic blanket. Maximize prescribed skin exposure and protect the eyes with correctly fitted shields.

Support frequent feeding. Track temperature, hydration, weight, urine, and stool because bilirubin leaves through stool and urine.

Document start/stop times, device, irradiance per protocol, interruptions, skin/eye checks, repeat bilirubin, and response.

FEED + DIAPERS

Cues—not a rigid clock.

  • Breastfeed responsively, commonly 8–12 times in 24 hours.
  • Formula needs vary; in the first days, many newborns take about 1–2 oz (30–60 mL) every 2–3 hours. Follow cues and the clinician’s plan.
  • Early diaper counts rise day by day. By day 5–7, expect about 6+ wets/day; stool frequency and texture vary with age and milk source.
  • Call for poor latch/suck, repeated vomiting, increasing sleepiness with feeds, or falling output.
BATH + SKIN + DIAPER

Warm. Gentle. Hands on.

  • Sponge-bathe until the cord stump falls off and the area heals.
  • Gather supplies first; keep one hand on the baby. Never leave the infant alone or place them under running water.
  • Use mild, fragrance-free products; newborns do not need a daily bath.
  • Change diapers promptly, cleanse gently, pat dry, and use fragrance-free petrolatum or zinc-oxide barrier when irritated.
SLEEP + TRAVEL

ABC + rear-facing.

  • Alone · Back · Crib: every sleep, on a firm flat approved surface with only a fitted sheet.
  • Room-share without bed-sharing; keep pillows, loose blankets, bumpers, toys, and weighted sleep products out.
  • Use a rear-facing car seat in the back seat until the seat’s height or weight limit is reached.
  • For installation help, use a certified Child Passenger Safety Technician or NHTSA inspection station; availability is not limited to fire departments.
FAMILY + FOLLOW-UP

Comfort builds trust.

  • Responding to newborn cries does not “spoil” the baby. Hold, feed, swaddle safely, talk, and learn the baby’s cues.
  • Supervise siblings closely. Use hand hygiene and avoid sick contacts; short outdoor time is fine when weather and the family’s condition allow.
  • Confirm screening results, medications/vaccines, follow-up appointment, feeding support, and a 24-hour contact number.
  • Ask about caregiver rest, mood, help at home, and safety. Thoughts of harming self or baby—or confusion, paranoia, or hallucinations—are an emergency.

WHEN TO GET HELP

Color. Breathing. Feeding. Fever. Output. Behavior.

EMERGENCY NOW

Call emergency services for severe breathing difficulty, blue/gray lips or tongue, unresponsiveness, seizure, or uncontrolled bleeding.

CALL IMMEDIATELY

Age 3 months or younger with a rectal temperature ≥100.4°F (38°C); hard to wake; poor feeding or repeated vomiting; fewer wet diapers; worsening jaundice; high-pitched/inconsolable cry; or redness, pus, foul odor, or active bleeding at the cord/procedure site.

MODULE 09 / BREAST + FORMULA FEEDING

Feed the baby. Read the cues.

Breast milk, expressed milk, and commercial infant formula can all be part of safe newborn care. The nurse protects intake, teaches technique, and keeps the plan practical and judgment-free.

NEWBORN UNIT09 / 13FEEDING CARE
FFollow cues

Hands to mouth, rooting, lip smacking, and clenched hands come before crying.

EEvaluate transfer

Look for a deep latch or coordinated bottle feed, rhythmic suck–swallow, and relaxed hands.

EExpress + store

Label, chill, thaw safely, and use the oldest milk first.

DDeliver safely

Mix formula exactly, hold the baby, pause often, and stop at fullness cues.

FEED: Follow cues · Evaluate transfer · Express + store · Deliver safely.

AT THE BREAST

Position → latch → transfer.

  • When parent and baby are stable, support skin-to-skin and an early feeding opportunity.
  • Align ear–shoulder–hip. Bring the baby to the breast: nose near nipple, mouth wide, chin touching first, lips flanged.
  • A good latch feels like tugging—not pinching. Listen for swallowing; the breast should soften and the baby’s hands relax.
  • Newborns commonly feed 8–12 times in 24 hours. Cluster feeding can be normal. Wake a sleepy newborn according to the weight/feeding plan.
WITH A BOTTLE

PACE: pause and follow.

  • Position semi-upright. Angle the bottle nearly horizontal. Check for pauses. End when full.
  • Keep the nipple filled enough to limit air, but let milk flow only when the baby sucks.
  • Fullness cues include relaxed hands, closing the mouth, slowing, or turning away. Never force a finished bottle.
  • Never prop a bottle, put the baby to bed with one, microwave it, or add cereal/food unless specifically prescribed.
MILK CHANGESColostrum → transitional → mature

Small amounts of concentrated colostrum fit the newborn stomach. Milk volume rises over the next days. “Foremilk” and “hindmilk” are a continuum—not two separate milks; fat generally rises as the breast empties.

VITAMIN D400 IU each day

Start shortly after birth for breastfed and mixed-fed infants. Formula-fed infants need supplementation until taking about 32 oz (1 L) of vitamin-D-fortified formula daily.

GOALRead the baby—not a recipe

Clinical calorie, protein, and fluid calculations are individualized. At the bedside, connect feeding quality with weight trend, diapers, jaundice, hydration, and behavior.

HIGH-RISK NEWBORNS

Powdered formula is not sterile. Ready-to-feed liquid is sterile and is the safest formula option for infants younger than 2 months, born prematurely, or immunocompromised when available. Follow facility and clinician guidance.

INTAKE CLUES

Transfer shows up later.

Watch the full feed, then trend weight and output. Wet diapers should increase day by day; by about day 5–7, 6 or more wets/day is a common reassuring clue.

CALL THE TEAM

Low intake can become urgent.

Escalate poor suck/latch, no audible swallowing, repeated vomiting, increasing jaundice, lethargy, dry mouth, falling urine output, or excessive/continuing weight loss. A rectal temperature ≥100.4°F (38°C) at 3 months or younger needs immediate medical evaluation.

SPECIAL PLANS

Contraindications are uncommon.

Classic galactosemia requires no human milk. PKU or MSUD needs specialist-directed feeding. Hepatitis B or C usually permits breastfeeding; HIV, active breast lesions, substance use, and medications require current individualized guidance—not a memorized blanket rule.

NURSING CLOSE-OUT

Observe one complete feed. Use teach-back or return demonstration. Document cues, position/latch or bottle technique, suck–swallow–breathe coordination, intake when measurable, tolerance, output, weight trend, teaching, and follow-up plan.

MODULE 10 / CONTRACEPTION

Choose by fit—not by myth.

Start with the patient’s pregnancy goals, medical eligibility, preferred amount of daily effort, bleeding preferences, and need for STI protection.

OB/GYN NOTES10 / 13CORE METHODS

Now, later, never, or unsure?

IIndividual safety

Use current U.S. MEC—not a memorized ban list.

CConvenience

Daily, per act, every few months, or set-and-forget?

KKeep STI protection

Condoms are the method that also lowers STI risk.

<1%IUD · IMPLANT

Most effective reversible choices. Rapid return to fertility after removal.

Progestin injection about every 3 months.

Effective when used on schedule; estrogen safety must be screened.

Typical-use pregnancy failure; also helps prevent STIs.

Rates are approximate first-year typical-use failure, not the chance of harm. Effectiveness is only one part of informed choice.

IUD QUICK COMPARE

Copper or levonorgestrel.

Copper: hormone-free; may increase bleeding/cramps. LNG: usually makes bleeding lighter and may lead to amenorrhea. Both are reversible and can often be placed immediately postpartum; immediate placement has a higher expulsion risk.

POSTPARTUM KEY

Ovulation can precede the first period.

IUDs and implants can often start before discharge. Progestin-only methods are broadly usable postpartum. Estrogen-containing pill, patch, or ring: wait and screen clot risk; they are not used in the first 21 days after birth.

LAM = ALL THREE

Under 6 · no period · nearly all breastfeeds.

Lactational amenorrhea works only while the baby is under 6 months, menstruation has not returned, and feeding is fully or nearly fully breastfeeding. If one changes, add another method.

NO ESTROGEN UNTIL CLEARED

Clot · aura · pressure · smoke.

Combined hormonal methods may be unsafe with early postpartum status, DVT/PE or thrombophilia, migraine with aura, uncontrolled hypertension, stroke/heart disease, or smoking at age 35 or older.

EMERGENCY CONTRACEPTIONASAP · WITHIN 5 DAYS

A copper IUD is highly effective and becomes ongoing contraception. Emergency pills include ulipristal or levonorgestrel; ulipristal works better than levonorgestrel on days 3–5. EC prevents pregnancy—it does not end an established pregnancy and does not protect against STIs.

NURSING FRAME

Use neutral, person-centered counseling. Confirm goals, contraindications, timing, backup needs, warning signs, follow-up, and the patient’s ability to stop or switch. Permanent contraception requires explicit, unpressured consent.

MODULE 11 / SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED INFECTIONS

Pattern first. Organism second.

Many infections are silent. Sort the clues, test the exposed sites, protect pregnancy and newborn care, then remember treatment, partners, and retesting.

OB/GYN NOTES11 / 13STI CORE
SScreen

Risk, pregnancy, and exposed sites.

TTreat

Patient—and partners when indicated.

OObstetric risk

Ask: placenta, birth canal, or newborn?

PPrevent

Condoms, vaccines, PrEP, and retesting.

DISCHARGE

Texture + odor + pH

  • Candida: thick white + itch; pH usually <4.5. Not an STI.
  • BV: thin gray/white + fishy; pH >4.5. Dysbiosis—not a classic STI.
  • Trich: frothy or malodorous discharge; STI. Treat partners.
CERVICITIS

Chlamydia + gonorrhea

  • Often asymptomatic; NAAT is the key test.
  • May cause discharge, bleeding, dysuria, or pelvic pain.
  • Untreated infection can ascend → PID, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, or chronic pain.
ULCERS

Painful HSV · painless syphilis

  • HSV: painful grouped vesicles/ulcers; antivirals control—not cure.
  • Syphilis: painless chancre; later rash can involve palms/soles.
  • Either HSV type can cause genital infection.
PREGNANCY / 4 FOR ALLHIV · HBV · HCV · SYPHILIS

Screen during every pregnancy. Add chlamydia and gonorrhea if younger than 25 or at increased risk; repeat testing follows risk and current guidance.

CTDoxycycline 7 days

Pregnancy: azithromycin. Test of cure at about 4 weeks; retest at 3 months.

GCCeftriaxone IM once

Add chlamydia treatment when it has not been excluded; retest at 3 months.

TRICHMetronidazole 7 days

For women: 500 mg twice daily. Treat partners; retest women at about 3 months.

SYPHPenicillin G

Only proven effective treatment in pregnancy. Allergy → desensitize, then penicillin.

PREGNANCY + BABY

Protect before delivery.

HSV lesions or prodrome at labor: cesarean birth is recommended; suppression usually begins at 36 weeks. HBsAg-positive parent: newborn gets hepatitis B vaccine + HBIG within 12 hours. ART can reduce perinatal HIV transmission to 1% or less.

PID / TREAT EARLY

Pelvic pain + tenderness.

Keep a low threshold when pelvic/lower abdominal pain comes with cervical-motion, uterine, or adnexal tenderness. Delay raises the risk of infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain.

VACCINE + PREVENTION

HPV · HBV · condoms · PrEP

HPV vaccine: 2 doses if started before 15; 3 if started at 15+ or immunocompromised. Catch-up through 26; discuss ages 27–45. Defer HPV vaccine during pregnancy. HIV U=U applies to sexual transmission with sustained viral suppression; PrEP prevents HIV.

NURSING CLOSE-OUT

Use neutral, confidential language. Collect blood, urine, vaginal/cervical, throat, or rectal samples based on exposure—not symptoms alone. Teach medication completion, partner care, when sex can safely resume, vaccination, condoms, retesting, and follow-up.

Comfort is a moving target.

Build the plan around the patient’s goals, culture, coping, labor pattern, and clinical status. Offer options without pressure—and expect the plan to change as labor changes.

SYSTEMIC OPIOID

Whole-body, partial relief.

IV/IM opioids reduce pain perception but may cause sedation, nausea, and respiratory depression. They cross the placenta, so timing is based on the expected birth—not a memorized dilation cutoff.

MONITOR: RR · SpO₂ · sedation · FHR
NITROUS OXIDE

Rapid on. Rapid off.

The patient holds the mask and stays conscious. It may reduce anxiety and make pain easier to cope with, but does not numb pain like an epidural.

DO NOT COMBINE WITH OPIOIDS/SEDATIVES

Local anesthetic, often with an opioid, runs through an epidural catheter. The patient remains awake and usually feels pressure. It may be started at any stage of labor when requested and clinically appropriate.

Follow facility and anesthesia protocols; no universal rule requires a routine 2-L fluid preload.

Side-lying or uterine displacement. Track BP and FHR. Carry out orders for IV fluid and vasopressor. Page anesthesia and the obstetric team; escalate urgently for severe symptoms or fetal bradycardia.

NEURAXIAL RED FLAGS

Refusal · infection · bleeding · instability.

Key barriers include patient refusal, infection at the insertion site, significant coagulopathy or unsafe anticoagulant timing, and major hemodynamic or neurologic concerns. Cardiac disease or prior spine surgery needs individualized anesthesia review—not an automatic “no.”

CALL ANESTHESIA NOW

Breathing, block, headache, or weakness.

Escalate dyspnea, rapidly rising numbness, profound hypotension, altered consciousness, seizure, tinnitus/metallic taste, severe back pain, new weakness, or a positional headache after neuraxial placement.

OPIOID + NEWBORN

Ventilation comes first.

If birth follows systemic opioid exposure, alert the newborn team. For neonatal respiratory depression, follow NRP and support ventilation; do not rely on routine delivery-room naloxone.

NURSING CLOSE-OUT

Pain score is only one clue. Document the patient’s goal, coping, chosen methods, response, maternal and fetal assessments, education, consent support, adverse effects, escalation, and the updated plan. A doula provides continuous nonclinical labor support—not midwifery care.

MODULE 13 / POSTPARTUM NURSING

Recover. Reassess. Respect the fourth trimester.

Postpartum recovery begins after the placenta is delivered, but care is not a single “6-week check.” Assess the whole person, teach the expected pattern, and keep watching for complications through the first year.

Find the source while treating the loss.

Do not wait for a perfect number. Cumulative blood loss of 1,000 mL or more, or bleeding with hypovolemia signs within 24 hours, is hemorrhage regardless of birth route.

PERINEUM + INCISION

Redness, edema, ecchymosis, drainage, approximation. Ice is useful early; later warmth may soothe. Severe pressure or one-sided swelling suggests hematoma.

Fluids, fiber, ambulation, and prescribed stool softener support bowel recovery. Early ambulation also lowers clot risk.

BREASTS

MOOD / BLUES ≠ DEPRESSION ≠ PSYCHOSIS

Duration, function, and reality testing separate them.

BABY BLUESStarts around days 2–3; better by 2 weeks.

Tearful, irritable, overwhelmed, but reality testing remains intact. Rest and support matter; reassess if intense or persistent.

DEPRESSION / ANXIETYPersistent, intense, or disrupts function.

Can begin during pregnancy or anytime in the first year. Screen with a validated tool; connect to treatment. It is treatable—not a failure.

POSTPARTUM PSYCHOSISEmergency.

Confusion, paranoia, mania, hallucinations, delusions, or unsafe thoughts. Do not leave parent alone with the infant; obtain emergency help now.

URGENT MATERNAL WARNING SIGNS / UP TO 1 YEAR

BLEED · BREATHE · BRAIN · BP · FEVER · LEG

BLEED: soaking a pad in an hour, clots larger than an egg, tissue, or foul discharge.

BREATHE: chest pain, shortness of breath, fast heartbeat, fainting.

BRAIN / BP: severe headache, vision change, seizure, RUQ pain, face/hand swelling, or high BP.

FEVER: 100.4°F / 38°C or higher.

LEG: one-sided arm or leg redness, swelling, warmth, or pain.

SAFETY: thoughts of harming self or baby, confusion, or hallucinations.

SEEK URGENT HELP. SAY: “I WAS PREGNANT WITHIN THE LAST YEAR.”

Earlier and more frequent follow-up may be needed for BP, incision, feeding, mood, or complications.

Choose contraception before risk returns. LAM works only when all 3 apply: under 6 months, no menses, and fully or nearly fully breastfeeding.

Return to sex is individualized around healing, bleeding, pain, desire, and the care plan. Use contraception and STI protection as needed.

NURSING CLOSE-OUT

Trend fundus, lochia, vital signs, pain, urine, incision/perineum, breasts, legs, and mood—not isolated findings. Document teaching, support, warning signs, follow-up, and the patient's understanding. A fever or tachycardia is never something to dismiss as routine postpartum recovery.
